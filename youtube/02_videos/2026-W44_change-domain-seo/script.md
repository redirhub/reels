## A · Meet Frosty Bites

1. [warm] Meet Frosty Bites. A little ice cream shop with a very loyal following.
2. Five years of scooping. Forty flavors. And one pistachio that people drive across town for.
3. [curious] Now, type "best pistachio ice cream" into Google, anywhere near their shop…
4. [proud] …and there they are. Right at the top.
5. That spot didn't happen by luck. It's SEO.
6. SEO, search engine optimization, is everything that helps Google decide your page is the best answer to a search.
7. For Frosty Bites, that's five years of it. Pages Google trusts. Food blogs that linked to their pistachio. Reviews that mention them by name.
8. [pause] And all of it is tied to one address: frostybites.com.

## B · The name problem

9. [amused] There was one small problem.
10. [laughs softly] A customer once asked if Frosty Bites was a dental clinic.
11. Another asked if they treated frostbite.
12. [sighs] Fair. It does sound a little… medical.
13. [excited] So, a new name. Velvet Scoop. Smooth, sweet, and nobody's booking a check-up. New domain: velvetscoop.com.
14. [pause] And then comes the thought that keeps every business owner up at night.
15. [worried] What happens to the Google spot? The pistachio page. The five years.
16. Change the domain the wrong way, and Google meets Velvet Scoop like a total stranger.
17. [reassuring] Do it right, and every ranking moves with you. Nothing starts from zero.
18. So how do you change your domain name without losing SEO? It comes down to three steps.

## C · How Google sees your site

19. First, the thing most people get wrong. Google doesn't rank your domain. It ranks your pages.
20. To Google, Frosty Bites is a list of addresses. The homepage. The flavor menu. And that famous page: frostybites.com/flavors/pistachio.
21. Each page has its own history. How long it has ranked, and who links to it.
22. Change the domain, and every one of those addresses changes too.
23. So the job isn't moving a domain. It's moving every page, in a way Google can follow.
24. Three steps. Map it. Redirect it. Tell Google.

## D · The three steps

25. Step one: the map.
26. Old address on the left, new address on the right. frostybites.com/flavors/pistachio becomes velvetscoop.com/flavors/pistachio.
27. If the paths stay the same, one rule covers every page. If some pages changed, each one gets its own line.
28. Quick tip: before you change anything, export your page list from your sitemap or from Google Search Console. That's your inventory.
29. [conspiratorial] Now, here's where most domain changes quietly go wrong.
30. Mapping every page feels like a chore. So people take a shortcut: send everything to the new homepage.
31. [light] And it looks perfect. Every old link lands somewhere. No errors. Pistachio fans arrive at velvetscoop.com.
32. [pause] But they wanted the pistachio. Now they're on a homepage, hunting for it.
33. [serious] And Google notices too. Hundreds of pages, all poured into one. It can treat them as pages that no longer exist.
34. [softly] And that top spot for "best pistachio ice cream"? It starts to melt.
35. [firm] One old page, one matching new page. That's the rule.
36. Step two: the redirects. Here's what that looks like in RedirHub.
37. Links. Create. Website Migration.
38. Old domain: frostybites.com. New domain: velvetscoop.com.
39. Same paths? One same-path rule, and every page forwards to its twin. The www version comes along too.
40. Changed pages? Paste your map, or upload it as a spreadsheet file, a CSV.
41. Before anything goes live, RedirHub shows you what it will add, and flags every row with a problem. Nothing is saved until you confirm.
42. Every redirect should be a 301, the permanent kind. That's the signal that tells Google to carry a page's history to its new address.
43. Then point frostybites.com at RedirHub with the records it gives you, and the padlock is set up automatically. Our earlier videos walk through that part.
44. Step three: tell Google you've moved.
45. Google Search Console is Google's free dashboard for your site. Open the old site there, and use Change of Address to point it at the new one.
46. Then submit the new sitemap: the list of pages you want Google to find.
47. And update everything you control. Your site's own links. Your Instagram bio. The QR code on the menu. Your Google Maps listing. All of it says Velvet Scoop now.

## E · The proof, the wobble, the keep

48. [curious] Now, test it the way Google sees it.
49. Open RedirHub's free Redirect Checker, and paste the old pistachio page.
50. [delighted] One hop. A 301. And it lands on velvetscoop.com/flavors/pistachio. That's a pass.
51. Try a few more. Your best sellers. An old blog post. The www version.
52. [gently] Then comes the part nobody warns you about. For a few weeks, your rankings may wobble.
53. [reassuring] That's Google re-reading your site at its new address. It's normal. Whatever you do, don't undo the redirects.
54. And RedirHub's analytics show which old addresses people still use, so you can watch the move happen.
55. Two rules before you go.
56. Keep the redirects for at least a year. Google says as long as possible. Honestly? Keep them for good.
57. [playful] And keep renewing frostybites.com. If it expires, someone else can buy it, along with every link that still points to it. And every pistachio fan who types it.
58. So. Map every page. Redirect each one to its twin with a 301. Tell Google. Then let it settle.
59. [warm] A few weeks later, search "best pistachio ice cream" again…
60. [proud] …and there's Velvet Scoop. Right where Frosty Bites used to be.
